Project 2 · Risk-based authentication

How do you stop account takeover without annoying good users?

Every login gets a 0–100 risk score. The policy then allows it, asks for a step-up challenge, or blocks it. A synthetic traffic generator plays 2,000 users and six attack types over 30 days, so we can measure what each threshold costs.

Default policy: step-up at score ≥ 30, block at ≥ 70, OTP challenge. 52,543 password-correct logins scored after a 7-day warm-up, including 199 takeover attempts. Seed 42.

What the simulation shows

Findings

  • Bulk attacks are easy. Credential stuffing from datacenters, brute force and impossible travel are all caught at 100%. Hosting ASNs, headless browsers and one-IP-many-accounts give them away.
  • VPN users are the false-positive problem. About 55% of their VPN logins get challenged, because they look like datacenter traffic from another country. Almost everyone else is under 1.5%. The obvious product fix: after a user passes a step-up on a VPN, trust that VPN provider for them.
  • The local attacker is the hard case. Valid password, home city, normal hours, residential IP: only 66% caught at the default policy. A new device is the only signal left.
  • The cost curve has a cliff. Raising the step-up threshold from 34 to 36 drops the catch rate from 91% to 74%. New-device-only attacks score in the low 30s, so a few points of threshold decide whether they get through.
  • Passkeys change the maths more than tuning does. At the same threshold, a passkey challenge cuts friction by a third and stops attackers who phish or relay OTP codes. Lowest cost drops from about $4.2k to $1.0k.

Good users: 99.6% of password-correct logins

Each user has a home city and ISP, 1–3 devices and a personal login-hour pattern. Realistic edge cases are built in on purpose, because without them every policy looks perfect:

  • Travellers (6%): real trips, no logins on the flight day
  • VPN users (7%): 40% of logins exit from datacenter IPs abroad
  • New phones (12%): primary device replaced mid-month
  • Shift workers (8%): normal logins at 1–4 am
  • Typos: 6% of logins have one failed attempt first

Attacks: six labelled scenarios

  • Credential stuffing: bots on datacenter/Tor IPs, 25–60 accounts per IP, 3% of passwords valid
  • Smart stuffing: rotating residential proxies, real browsers
  • Brute force: one account, 15–40 guesses
  • Impossible travel: valid password used more than 3,000 km away, minutes after the real user
  • New device at 3am: same country, victim's local night
  • Local attacker: same city, normal hours, residential IP

How the metrics are defined

  • Only password-correct attempts count. A wrong password fails whatever the engine decides, so the decision only matters when the password is right.
  • Catch rate: blocked, or stepped up and failing the challenge. OTP lets about 10% of attackers through (phishing kits, SIM swap). Passkeys let none through.
  • False-positive rate: good logins stepped up or blocked. Blocks are also reported on their own because they cost far more.
  • Friction: seconds added per good login. OTP 30s, passkey 6s, block 300s (account recovery).
Research grounding

What the design is based on

  • Wiefling et al., risk-based authentication studies. They tested how large online services decide when to challenge a login. A few features carry most of the signal: IP/ASN, device and location. Their team also published a public dataset of about 33M login attempts, based on a real service, which is the best check for this simulator. RBA dataset on Kaggle
  • OWASP Credential Stuffing Prevention Cheat Sheet. It recommends layered defences: MFA, device fingerprinting, IP reputation and rate limits per IP and per account. These are the signals used here. cheatsheetseries.owasp.org
  • NIST SP 800-63B. It requires rate limiting of failed attempts and treats phishing-resistant authenticators such as passkeys as stronger than OTP codes. That's why the simulator models different attacker pass rates for OTP and passkeys. pages.nist.gov
  • Industry products such as Okta, Auth0 and Microsoft Entra use the same allow / challenge / deny bands with configurable sensitivity. The threshold slider above is that configuration setting.

    This explorer, fed by real app traffic, with the reasons behind each decision shown per login.

  6. Model and passkeys

    Logistic regression vs. rules, tested on an attack mix it wasn't trained on; WebAuthn passkeys via py_webauthn.

Limits of the current numbers

Read these before trusting the results

  • Every simulated attack comes from a new device, so a strict passkey policy reaches 100%. Real attackers also use stolen session cookies and malware on the victim's own device. Those scenarios come next.
  • The sweep re-applies thresholds to fixed scores. User history is learned once, under the default policy.
  • The generator encodes my assumptions about attackers. A model trained on it would mostly learn the generator. The public RBA dataset is the check for that.
